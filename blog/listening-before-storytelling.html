Listening before storytelling

The strongest development stories do not begin with a camera or a content plan. They begin with attention.

During my work in development communications, I wrote more than fifty human-interest stories. The most important part of that work rarely appeared in the final copy: the time spent listening.

People are not proof points

Communications work often arrives with a framework already in place—an outcome to demonstrate, a campaign message to reinforce, a report to complete. Those needs are real, but they can flatten a person’s experience into evidence for an institutional story.

Listening changes the direction of travel. It leaves room for contradiction, hesitation and context. It asks not only “what happened?” but “what does this mean to you?”

A good story does not borrow someone’s voice. It creates the conditions for that voice to remain recognisably theirs.

Clarity with consent

Ethical storytelling is also practical. It means explaining where material will appear, avoiding promises a programme cannot keep, checking names and details, and recognising when a story should not be published at all. The responsibility continues through editing, captioning and image selection.

Communication as infrastructure

At its best, development communication is not decoration added after the work. It helps communities understand services, enables teams to learn across locations and gives decision-makers a clearer picture of lived outcomes. That requires craft—but also patience, humility and accountability.

These reflections grew from communications and documentation work across education, child-marriage prevention and community programmes in India.
