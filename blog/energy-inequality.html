What a fuel shock reveals about inequality

Energy security is often described at the scale of nations. But the effects of disruption are lived at the scale of households.

A national energy balance can look stable while families experience something entirely different. That tension became the starting point for my MSc research.

The average is not the experience

Looking across government energy data, I compared three policy cases and followed the effects of a major supply disruption through fuel consumption, prices, employment and food provisioning. The clearest divergence appeared between petrol and LPG: one continued to grow while the other contracted sharply.

The comparison matters because fuels sit inside different social systems. Petrol is linked to mobility and commercial activity. LPG is tied closely to household cooking, care work and time. A fall in cooking-fuel access is not only an energy statistic; it can mean a return to dirtier fuels, more unpaid labour and greater exposure to indoor air pollution.

Distribution is not a footnote to energy security. It is the question.

Following the pathways

I used Python, pandas and reproducible visualisations to test the pattern against alternative explanations. Price data, employment composition, food-provisioning systems and time-use evidence helped distinguish what the dataset could establish from what required careful interpretation.

The work reinforced a principle I want to carry into future research: models are most useful when they make their assumptions visible. Scenario analysis should clarify uncertainty, not disguise it.

What I am taking forward

Climate transition debates need technical ambition, but they also need a grounded account of who absorbs risk. That means examining access, care, labour and public provisioning alongside supply volumes and investment.

This note draws on Energy Security, Climate Transition and Social Equity: India’s Response to the 2026 Strait of Hormuz Disruption, completed at SOAS University of London.
